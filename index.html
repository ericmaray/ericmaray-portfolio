01 — About

A bit about me.

My career hasn't followed a particularly conventional path. I've worked across disciplines that, on paper, don't seem especially connected. I've never seen them that way.

Each experience has changed how I understand people, processes, and the systems they operate within. What I've learned in one environment often becomes unexpectedly useful in another. And somewhere along the way, I've found myself contributing to conversations and solving problems that don't necessarily fall within my job description.

I'm drawn to complexity, but not for the sake of making things complicated. I like understanding how things work, questioning why they're done a certain way, and connecting ideas that might otherwise remain separate.

I don't expect to have all the answers. I'd rather ask better questions, challenge my own assumptions, and keep learning from people who see things differently.

I've learned that the problem being discussed isn't always the problem that needs solving. Sometimes the most valuable contribution is simply helping people see something they hadn't considered before.

Self-awareness matters

I take self-awareness seriously — partly because I ask it of the people I work with, and partly because it makes me better at what I do. I recently completed Ryan Gottfredson's Personal Mindset Assessment, benchmarked against 50,000+ respondents. The results were honest — strong across three of four dimensions, and a useful reminder that there's always more to develop. The one that wasn't in the top quartile: growth mindset. For someone who believes deeply in learning, that's a useful thing to sit with.

02 — Philosophy

Three pillars.

What I keep coming back to.

Three ideas that have shaped how I approach this work. Less a manifesto, more a set of reminders — the things I find myself returning to when a program isn't quite landing.

I · Servitium

Selfless Service

Learning exists to serve people — their growth, their confidence, their capacity to do meaningful work. That orientation shapes every design decision I make.

II · Curiositas

Behavioral Curiosity

Understanding why people do what they do — and what it takes to shift that — is the lens through which I approach every needs assessment, curriculum, and facilitation.

III · Ratio

Systems Thinking

Individual learning experiences don't change organizations. Systems do. I design with the ecosystem in mind — how learning connects to culture, structure, and long-term capability.

03 — A Story

Meet Odin.

What my dog taught me
about learning.

Odin is a double merle. He's completely deaf and nearly completely blind.

The rescue had a hard time finding him a home — separation anxiety, chewing, not house-trained. We brought him home anyway.

I'd trained dogs before, including a few with disabilities, but Odin was different. So I spent time just watching — trying to understand his world before I tried to change anything in it. Eventually it became clear that touch was the channel he could actually receive. That's where we built from.

He responds to commands now as well as my other dogs, who have all their senses. They've learned to communicate with him through touch, too — which I didn't teach them. They figured it out on their own.

Not everyone learns the same way. Not everyone has the same capabilities. But everyone wants to be the best version of themselves.

— Something Odin keeps reminding me.
A few things this has reinforced
  • —

    Get to know the learner before you design the lesson.

  • —

    Build around what someone can do, not what we expect them to.

  • —

    The system has to adapt too. Communication goes both ways.

04 — Personal

Beyond the work.

I grew up in Fayetteville, North Carolina — which means I grew up around the military before I ever chose it. My family has had someone in uniform in every major conflict the United States has been part of. Service wasn't something I decided to do. It was something I was raised inside of.

I later served two tours in Operation Enduring Freedom with the 101st Airborne Division. That chapter is a significant part of who I am.

Growing up I played soccer, wrestled, ran track, and played baseball. I was competitive — genuinely passionate about getting better, and stubborn enough to outwork the people around me. Oddly, I didn't apply that same energy to school. Not because I didn't love learning — I did — but because I struggled with being told how I was supposed to learn. Conforming to a standard format for something as personal as learning never made sense to me. It still doesn't. That tension is probably why I ended up in L&D.

There's something about bison that I find useful to think about. When a storm comes, most animals turn and run — which means they stay inside it longer. Bison turn into it. They move through it faster by going directly at what most things avoid. I don't know that I chose that approach consciously, but looking back at the decisions that shaped my path, it's a fair description of the pattern.

My career hasn't followed a straight line. It's been more of a lattice than a ladder — a lot of sideways moves that turned out to be the right ones, mostly because I said yes to things other people passed on. That comes from the same place as the service orientation: a willingness to go where you're needed, even when it's uncomfortable.

I'm a husband to Cassandra and a father to two boys. For a long time now, becoming better — as a professional, as a person — has been something I do for them as much as for myself. They're the reason the work has stakes.

02 / Leadership Development

Developing leaders before the promotion

CoServ · Multi-year initiative

I made the formal case to leadership, earned the buy-in, and built a development track that started before the promotion — so the first day in a new seat wasn't also the first day learning how to lead.

04 / Safety

Four years without a major incident

Zero major OSHA incidents over four consecutive years at a fast-growing utility. The field crews deserve most of that credit. My contribution was helping shift safety training from an annual event into something woven into how people worked day to day.
